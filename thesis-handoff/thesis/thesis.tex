\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
A user-interface (UI) transition graph describes the screens of an application and the transitions a
user can cause between them. For single-page web applications such graphs are usually
built by running the application. Client-side routing frameworks now declare routes in the
source code, which makes static extraction possible. How well static extraction works on
real applications, and why it fails, has not been measured construct by construct.

This thesis makes that measurement. It extracts transition graphs from six open-source
Angular applications and checks them with five methods: four read the source code and one
reads the route configuration of the running application. Every navigation construct the
extractor does not recover is assigned to a loss category and given a recorded reason. Under
the order in which the loss rules are applied, the largest loss is navigation that belongs to
no single screen, such as a link in a shared header. For this loss the thesis proposes scope
attribution, a dataflow analysis that computes the set of screens from which each navigation
site can fire.

Three results stand out. First, almost no navigation can be extracted as a deterministic
transition. The corpus yields \CensusMust{} such edges from \CensusMustSites{} constructs. Most
of them come from one toolbar whose screen set is untested, and most rest on an assumption
about route order that the extractor does not read. Second, every defect class traces to the
extractor rather than to the applications. Almost every cause behind those classes was a
construct the extractor could read and did not, and most of these were repaired; two causes
lie beyond any static reader of a route table. Third, scope attribution recovers
\AttrRecoveredShare{} of the constructs the loss rules assign to it, almost all as possible
rather than certain transitions. On the one application whose screen sets were read by hand,
it adds almost nothing to a reimplementation of earlier mechanisms. That reference applies the
analysis's own relations, was partly completed from the graph's output and guided two
repairs. Mutation testing on three applications shows that the graph responds to injected
changes in the constructs it represents.

The conclusion is that a declared route table is a usable starting point for static
navigation extraction, but not a sufficient one. Scope attribution adds a modest gain over
earlier mechanisms, and its screen sets were checked in both directions on one application
only. The corpus is small and Angular-only, and the results describe these six
applications.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} static analysis; UI transition graph; single-page
application; Angular; dataflow analysis; program analysis; software testing

\chapter*{Use of AI tools}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Use of AI tools}

Aalto University asks that the use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in a thesis be
declared. An AI coding assistant was used in this work in the ways set out below, and I am
responsible for the whole of the result.

The extractor under study and the evaluation harness were written with the help of an AI
coding assistant. The result tables and plots of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are generated from
the harness's result files, except the two tables marked as compiled by hand. So are the figures
the text states for the final instrument. All of these can be reproduced from the commits
recorded with them. Four kinds of figure cannot:
\begin{itemize}
\item those of superseded instruments, copied by hand from the outputs of those runs
  (Appendix~\ref{app:validation});
\item a few counts taken at named earlier commits whose output was not committed;
\item a few counts from the history of the structural reference and of the instruments, stated
  in words;
\item the corpus-selection counts of Section~\ref{sec:corpus}, read from the candidate
  repositories.
\end{itemize} The diagrams in
Chapters~\ref{ch:introduction} to~\ref{ch:evaluation} were drawn by hand.

The assistant was also used to read the application sources, in three roles. As a reviewer it
read the constructs behind an edge and reported where the extractor's reading disagreed with
them. In red-team panels it attacked the extractor, the harness and this text. Several of the
demotion rules of Section~\ref{sec:res-round2} were written in response to its objections, among
them the rule by which the extractor decides which guards a navigation runs. Every defect it
reported in either role was reproduced against the code before anything was changed, and the
corrections that followed are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} as corrections. Its readings
are also part of the evaluation itself. The two readings of the intermediate instrument's must
tier in \path{results-must/review.json} and the reading of the final tier in
\path{results-must/tier-reading.json} were made by the assistant. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} says of
each what it can and cannot show, given who made it. The two readings are two runs of one model,
so their agreement measures consistency, not correctness.

The assistant was also used during the literature search, to locate candidate sources and
to summarise them. No source is cited on the strength of such a summary. Every entry in
the bibliography was checked field by field against the publisher page, Crossref
metadata, arXiv metadata or DBLP before it was cited, and sources that could not be
checked in this way are kept in a separate block in the bibliography file and are not
cited anywhere in the text.

Finally, the assistant was used to draft and edit prose, which I then revised. The
research questions and the interpretation of the results are my own; the analysis and the
evaluation protocol were revised in the course of the work in response to the reviews and panels
described above, as Chapter~\ref{ch:results} records, and the responsibility for every claim
made here is mine.
