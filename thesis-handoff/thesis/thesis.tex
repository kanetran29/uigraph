\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
A UI transition graph describes the screens of an application and the transitions a
user can cause between them. Earlier work extracts such graphs statically from the route
declarations of client-side web applications, but none that this review found
classifies what the extraction fails to read across a corpus. This thesis makes that measurement on six
open-source Angular applications with \RoundTwoRoutesRealworld{} to \RoundTwoRoutesMifos{}
declared route entries, with four methods that read the sources and one that reads the
running application.

The main result is that the deterministic tier is nearly empty. Once the demotion rules the
soundness property rests on are applied, \CensusMustSites{} constructs yield \CensusMust{}
must edges; \CensusMustSitesKdash{} of those constructs, one application's toolbar, account
for \CensusMustKdash{} of the edges, and \RouteOrderResting{} edges rest on an assumption
about route order the extractor does not read. The extractor mints a node for every
declared screen two separately written static readers find, but a runtime check of the route
configuration finds \ProbeMissed{} mounted addresses with no node, \ProbeMissedGhostfolio{}
of them in one application. Every lost construct is assigned to a defect class; after the
node-level defects were repaired, attribution of a navigation site to the screens it fires
from is the largest class in every application, and almost every class traces to a construct the
extractor could read and did not.

The thesis therefore formulates scope attribution as a dataflow analysis over screen sets,
a fixpoint over component nesting, router outlets and a call graph that accounts for
dependency injection. Its contribution beyond earlier work is modest. The mechanisms
earlier work has (the root component on every screen, plus the enclosing routes) reach
\GTOutletRecall{} of a hand-read structural reference of (site, screen) pairs on \GTApp{}, against
\GTFullRecall{} for the full analysis; across the corpus the call graph, its fallback join and
stand-ins add \AblationFullAddedSites{} placed sites, \AblationFullAddedShare{} of the total. Attribution recovers \AttrRecoveredShare{} of the constructs in its
class, almost all as possible rather than certain transitions.
Scopes are compared in both directions only on \GTApp{}, against a reference that applies the
analysis's own relations and guided two repairs; elsewhere the final scopes are unchecked, which
also bears on the sources of must edges.

On three applications, the graph detects \MutFallibleDetected{} of \MutFallibleScored{}
injected link deletions, retargets and guard removals; without attribution fewer changes can be
scored at all, but on the \MutPaired{} both graphs score the rates are almost the same. The
corpus is small and Angular-only, and the figures describe these six applications only.

\chapter*{Use of AI tools}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Use of AI tools}

Aalto University asks that the use of artificial intelligence tools in a thesis be
declared. An AI coding assistant was used in this work in the ways set out below, and I am
responsible for the whole of the result.

The extractor under study and the evaluation harness were written with the help of an AI
coding assistant. Every number reported in this thesis is produced by running that
harness, and the result tables and plots of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are generated from
its result files, except the two tables marked as compiled by hand, so that any number can
be reproduced from the commits recorded with it. The diagrams in
Chapters~\ref{ch:introduction} to~\ref{ch:evaluation} were drawn by hand.

The assistant was also used to read the application sources, in three roles. As a reviewer it
read the constructs behind an edge and reported where the extractor's reading disagreed with
them. In red-team panels it attacked the extractor, the harness and this text, and several of
the demotion rules of Section~\ref{sec:res-round2}, the rule by which the extractor decides which
guards a navigation runs among them, were written in response to its objections. Every defect it
reported in either role was reproduced against the code before anything was changed, and the
corrections that followed are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} as corrections. Its readings
are also part of the evaluation itself: the two readings of the round's must tier in
\path{results-must/review.json} and the reading of the final tier in
\path{results-must/tier-reading.json} were made by the assistant, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} says
of each what it can and cannot show, given who made it.

The assistant was also used during the literature search, to locate candidate sources and
to summarise them. No source is cited on the strength of such a summary. Every entry in
the bibliography was checked field by field against the publisher page, Crossref
metadata, arXiv metadata or DBLP before it was cited, and sources that could not be
checked in this way are kept in a separate block in the bibliography file and are not
cited anywhere in the text.

Finally, the assistant was used to draft and edit prose, which I then revised. The
research questions and the interpretation of the results are my own; the analysis and the
evaluation protocol were revised in the course of the work in response to the reviews and panels
described above, as Chapter~\ref{ch:results} records, and the responsibility for every claim
made here is mine.
