\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\vspace{-1.5em}
A user-interface (UI) transition graph describes the screens of an application and the transitions a
user can cause between them. For single-page web applications such graphs are usually built by running
the application, although routing frameworks declare routes in the source. How far static extraction
gets on real applications, and why it fails, has not been measured construct by construct.

This thesis builds a static extractor for Angular and measures it on six open-source applications,
checking the graphs in five ways and assigning every construct it does not recover to a loss category.
The largest category is navigation that belongs to no single screen, such as a link in a shared header.
For it the thesis proposes scope attribution, a dataflow analysis that computes the screens from which
each navigation site can fire.

Few transitions are certain. The corpus yields \RouteOrderMust{} edges that always fire, \RouteOrderMustWithout{}
without an assumed route order and \GuardOnlyMust{} if every guard admits the user; this is largely a
matter of definition, since \MayAppAny{} of the \MayOnlyConstructs{} constructs that stay merely possible
sit behind a guard or branch of the application. Most causes of loss traced in the source are
constructs a static reader can read. Scope attribution recovers \AttrRecoveredShare{} of the
constructs assigned to it, mostly on one application and almost all as possible transitions, and places
navigation inside services, which the published rules of earlier work do not. Its screen sets are
\BaselineFactorMin{} to \BaselineFactorMax{} times narrower than putting every site on every screen, but
against a reference read from one application's source its precision is \GTAlfFullPrecision{}. Beside
Rima et al.'s tool, on five corpus applications it finds \HHCorpusNoMifosOursRecall{} of the real
transitions either finds, against \HHCorpusNoMifosTheirsRecall{}, at precision
\HHCorpusNoMifosOursPrecision{} against \HHCorpusNoMifosTheirsPrecision{}, a grouping chosen after reading;
on the largest application its precision is \HHMifosOursPrecision{}. Mutation testing shows that the graph
responds to changes in constructs it represents, though not independently of the tool.

A declared route table is a usable but insufficient basis for static navigation extraction. The tool was
developed on this corpus, so the figures are in-sample, and whether the graph helps its intended
consumers is not measured.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} static analysis; UI transition graph; single-page
application; Angular; dataflow analysis; program analysis; software testing

\chapter*{Use of AI tools}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Use of AI tools}

Aalto University asks that the use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in a thesis be
declared. An AI coding assistant was used in this work in the ways set out below, and I am
responsible for the whole of the result.

The extractor built in this thesis and the evaluation harness were written with the help of an AI
coding assistant. The result tables and plots of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are generated from
the harness's result files, except the two tables marked as compiled by hand. So are the figures
the text states for the final version. All of these can be reproduced from the commits
recorded with them. Four kinds of figure cannot:
\begin{itemize}
\item those of superseded instruments, copied by hand from the outputs of those runs
  (Section~\ref{app:validation});
\item a few counts taken at named earlier commits whose output was not committed;
\item a few counts from the history of the structural reference and of the instruments, stated
  in words;
\item the corpus-selection counts of Section~\ref{sec:corpus}, read from the candidate
  repositories.
\end{itemize} The diagrams in
Chapters~\ref{ch:introduction} to~\ref{ch:evaluation} were drawn by hand.

The assistant was also used to read the application sources, in three roles. As a reviewer it
read the constructs behind an edge and reported where the extractor's reading disagreed with
them. In red-team panels it attacked the extractor, the harness and this text. Several of the
demotion rules of Section~\ref{sec:res-round2} were written in response to its objections, among
them the rule by which the extractor decides which guards a navigation runs. Every defect it
reported in either role was reproduced against the code before anything was changed, and the
corrections that followed are listed in Section~\ref{app:repairs}. Its readings
are also part of the evaluation itself. The two readings of a development version's must
tier in \path{results-must/review.json} and the reading of the final tier in
\path{results-must/tier-reading.json} were made by the assistant. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} says what
the reading of the final tier can and cannot show, and Table~\ref{tab:repairs} what the review of
the development version's tier found. The two readings are two runs of one model,
so their agreement measures consistency, not correctness.

The assistant was also used during the literature search, to locate candidate sources and
to summarise them. No source is cited on the strength of such a summary. Every entry in
the bibliography was checked field by field against the publisher page, Crossref
metadata, arXiv metadata or DBLP before it was cited, and sources that could not be
checked in this way are kept in a separate block in the bibliography file and are not
cited anywhere in the text.

Finally, the assistant was used to draft and edit prose, which I then revised. The
research questions and the interpretation of the results are my own; the analysis and the
evaluation protocol were revised in the course of the work in response to the reviews and panels
described above, as Section~\ref{app:repairs} records, and the responsibility for every claim
made here is mine.
